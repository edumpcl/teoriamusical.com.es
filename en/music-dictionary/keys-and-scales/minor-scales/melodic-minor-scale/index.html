Melodic Minor Scales

The melodic minor scale is built from the natural minor scale by raising the sixth and seventh degrees by a half step (UK: semitone) when the scale goes up. When it goes down, those two degrees return to their natural minor values. This double behavior smooths out the augmented second that the harmonic minor scale creates.

Why does the melodic minor scale exist?

It helps to start with the problem it solves. The natural minor scale has a lowered seventh degree (G♮ in A minor), which weakens the pull toward the tonic. The harmonic minor scale fixes this by raising the seventh degree (G♯), which creates a leading tone (UK: leading note) that resolves up to A. But that change causes a new problem: between the sixth degree (F) and the raised seventh (G♯) there are three half steps. This interval is an augmented second, and it is awkward to sing.

The solution is to raise the sixth degree as well when going up (F → F♯). Now F♯ to G♯ is an ordinary whole step (UK: tone), and the melody rises smoothly. When going down, the leading tone is no longer needed, because the melody is moving away from the tonic. Both degrees go back to their natural values.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a melodic minor scale?
The melodic minor scale is a variant of the natural minor scale in which the sixth and seventh degrees are raised by a half step when going up. Going down, the original natural minor degrees come back. This two-way form smooths out the augmented second of the harmonic minor scale.
How is the melodic minor scale different from the harmonic minor?
The harmonic minor raises only the seventh degree, which creates an augmented second between the sixth and seventh degrees. The melodic minor also raises the sixth degree when going up, which removes that leap and makes the melody flow. Going down, the melodic minor returns to the natural minor degrees, while the harmonic minor keeps the raised seventh in both directions.
Why does the melodic minor scale change when it goes down?
Going down, the pull of the leading tone toward the tonic is no longer needed, because the melody is moving away from the tonic. So the natural degrees come back, and they sound smoother in a descending line.
What are the notes of the A melodic minor scale?
Ascending, A melodic minor has the notes A, B, C, D, E, F♯, G♯, A. Descending, it returns to the natural minor: A, G, F, E, D, C, B, A.
Is the melodic minor scale the same in jazz and in classical music?
No. In classical theory the ascending and descending forms are different. In jazz, the ascending form is used in both directions (the jazz melodic minor). It is the same as a major scale with a lowered third degree.
