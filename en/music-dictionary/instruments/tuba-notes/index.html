Tuba Notes

The tuba is the lowest of the brass instruments: the foundation that the harmony of the concert band and the orchestra rests on. Like the trumpet, it picks a note by combining valves with the harmonics produced by the lips, but it sounds much lower and usually has four valves. Tubas are built in C, B♭, E♭ and F. This page shows the fingerings of the tuba in C, which is read at concert pitch in bass clef (it does not transpose), so the note you read is the note you hear.

How are the notes produced? Valves and harmonics

The sound starts at the lips, which vibrate against the mouthpiece and produce a series of harmonics. The valves fill the gaps between those harmonics: each one adds tubing and lowers the pitch, exactly as on the trumpet, but starting from C.

1st + 2nd + 4th8 half steps

For example, the open harmonics of a tuba in C include C2, G2, C3, E3 and G3. Press the 4th valve on the open G2 and you get D2, a perfect 4th lower.

The fourth valve: the low register

The 4th valve lowers the instrument by a perfect 4th. It does the same job as 1st + 3rd, but it is better in tune: the 1st + 3rd combination leaves the tubing slightly short, so the note tends to sound sharp, while the 4th valve adds the length that is missing. It is the key to the tuba's low register: combined with the other three valves it reaches the lowest notes (the table below goes down to E1 on four valves) and gives alternate fingerings that are better in tune. Three-valve tubas do not go as low, which is why four valves are standard today. Professional tubas often have five or six, which extend the low range and improve intonation further.

C, B♭, E♭ and F: the types of tuba

There is not just one tuba. The most common ones are:

  • Tuba in C (CC): the one on this page. Widely used in orchestras and bands; read at concert pitch in bass clef.
  • Tuba in B♭ (BB♭): the classic band bass, a whole step lower than the tuba in C. Its fingerings are different.
  • Tuba in E♭ (EE♭): very common in bands, a perfect 4th higher than the tuba in B♭.
  • Tuba in F: higher and more agile than the tuba in C (a perfect 4th above it). Often used as a second tuba and for solo work.

The valves can be piston valves or rotary valves. The mechanism changes, but the fingering logic is the same.

Is the tuba a transposing instrument?

It depends on the tradition. In orchestras and in most wind bands (including the Spanish and US bands), the tuba is read at concert pitch in bass clef, whatever its tuning: the player uses the fingerings of their own instrument for the notes that sound. The tuba in C on this page needs no transposition at all. In the British brass band tradition, in contrast, the E♭ and B♭ basses are written in treble clef as transposing instruments: the E♭ bass sounds a major 13th (an octave plus a major 6th) below the written note, and the B♭ bass a major 16th (two octaves plus a whole step) below it. It is the same instrument, read in a different way.

The tuba in the band and the orchestra

The tuba is the base of the sound. In the concert band, which is the backbone of the Valencian tradition, it supports the harmony and plays the bass line of the pasodoble and the march. In the orchestra it anchors the brass section together with the bass trombone. Its large conical tube gives a round, deep sound that can fill a whole hall from the lowest register.

A symphonic band usually has one to four tubas, depending on its size. An orchestra normally has just one (some works ask for two). In everyday writing a tuba in C covers roughly E1 to F4 on four valves, and professionals extend it at both ends: the fifth and sixth valves and pedal tones go lower still. One fact that surprises many people: although it is the lowest brass instrument, its unwound tubing is about five meters long (around 16 ft) on a tuba in C, and longer on a tuba in B♭ (about 5.5 meters).

Common mistakes when learning tuba notes

  • Thinking there is one fingering per note. As on the trumpet, there are few combinations: the same fingering gives several notes depending on the harmonic.
  • Ignoring the 4th valve in the low register. Many low notes need the 4th valve (alone or combined); without it they are out of tune or do not speak at all.
  • Mixing up the fingerings of different tubas. The fingerings of the tuba in C do not work on a tuba in B♭ or F; use the ones for your own instrument.
  • Neglecting the air. The tuba needs a large, steady flow of air; a powerful, in-tune low register comes from the air column, not from forcing the lips.

In short

  • The tuba is the bass of the brass: valves plus harmonics, like the trumpet but much lower, usually with four valves.
  • It comes in C, B♭, E♭ and F. This page shows the tuba in C, read at concert pitch in bass clef (no transposition).
  • The 4th valve (a perfect 4th down) opens the low register and gives better-tuned alternatives.
  • It is the harmonic foundation of the band and the orchestra.

Frequently Asked Questions

What key is the tuba in?
There are several: the tuba in C (CC), in B♭ (BB♭), in E♭ and in F (higher than the tuba in C). Each has its own fingerings. This page shows the ones for the tuba in C, which is widely used in orchestras and bands. The tuba in C is read at concert pitch in bass clef, so it does not transpose.
Does the tuba transpose?
Usually not. In orchestras and most wind bands the tuba is written at concert pitch in bass clef, so what you read is what you hear. In the British brass band tradition the E♭ and B♭ basses are instead written in treble clef as transposing instruments: the E♭ bass sounds a major 13th below the written note and the B♭ bass a major 16th below it.
What is the fourth valve of the tuba for?
The 4th valve lowers the pitch by a perfect 4th (it equals 1st + 3rd, but is better in tune). It does two things: it reaches the lowest notes, completing the register below the open harmonics, and it offers better-tuned alternate fingerings in the low register.
How many valves does a tuba have?
Four is the most common today, although there are tubas with three (simpler) and with five or six (professional models, with more options in the low register). The valves can be pistons or rotary valves; the fingering system is the same.
