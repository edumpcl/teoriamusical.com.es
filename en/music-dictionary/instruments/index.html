The Families of Musical Instruments

Musical instruments are grouped into families according to how they produce sound. In the symphony orchestra, four large groups are usually distinguished: strings, woodwinds, brass and percussion. In wind bands (concert bands), winds and percussion dominate. To study instruments in more detail, this dictionary uses seven families: it splits the strings into bowed and plucked, the percussion into pitched and unpitched, and adds the keyboard as a practical group.

On this page you will find the complete map: the seven families with a photo of each, how instruments are classified by the way they sound, and where each family appears in an orchestra, in a band and in the score.

  • Woodwinds: the air enters through a mouthpiece, an edge or a reed, and keys open or close holes. Flute, oboe, clarinet, bassoon and saxophone.
  • Brass: the sound starts when the lips vibrate in a mouthpiece; valves, rotors or a slide change the length of the tube. Trumpet, horn, trombone and tuba.
  • Bowed strings: a bow makes the string vibrate and the note can be sustained. Violin, viola, cello and double bass.
  • Plucked strings: the string is plucked with the fingers or a pick and the sound dies away. Guitar, harp, mandolin and banjo.
  • Keyboard: they are played with keys, but each one sounds in a different way. Piano, organ, harpsichord, accordion, synthesizer and celesta.
  • Pitched percussion: they are struck and give definite notes. Xylophone, marimba, vibraphone, glockenspiel and timpani.
  • Unpitched percussion: they are struck, shaken or scraped and are used mostly to produce sounds without a definite pitch. Snare drum, bass drum, cymbals, triangle and maracas.

How instruments are classified: by how they sound

There are two ways to group instruments. The traditional one, used in the orchestra and the band, is the seven families above. The scientific one is the Hornbostel–Sachs system (1914), which looks at the element that produces the sound:

CategoryElement that produces the soundExamples
ChordophonesA stringViolin, guitar, harp, piano
AerophonesA column of air set in vibrationFlute, clarinet, trumpet, organ, accordion
MembranophonesA membrane or drumheadSnare drum, bass drum, timpani, congas
IdiophonesThe body of the instrument itselfCymbals, triangle, xylophone, marimba, castanets
ElectrophonesThe electronic generation or processing of soundSynthesizer, electronic organ

The two systems answer different questions. Hornbostel–Sachs groups instruments by the element that produces the sound and the way it is set in motion. That is why the violin and the piano are both chordophones (in the piano, strings sound, even though hammers strike them), and the organ and the accordion are aerophones, because their sound is made by air. The traditional classification, on the other hand, uses practical families such as woodwind, brass or bowed strings, which are very common in music study and in the organization of ensembles.

And a frequent trap: “wood” and “brass” do not describe the material. The modern flute is made of metal and is counted among the woodwinds; the saxophone is also made of metal, and it is a woodwind because it sounds with a reed.

Notice a difference you can hear: the orchestra rests on the bowed strings, while the band is built on winds and percussion, which is why the repertoire of each sounds so different.

The families in the score

In orchestral and band scores, the instruments usually appear grouped in a conventional order, which can vary with the ensemble and the work. In an orchestral score, the woodwinds are usually placed above the brass and percussion, while the strings normally occupy the bottom. Knowing the families helps you find each instrument in the score.

Within each family, the instruments usually follow a standard order related to their register, from highest to lowest: for example, in the woodwinds, piccolo, flute, oboe, clarinet and bassoon. And each family has its own writing habits: many brass and several woodwind instruments are transposing instruments, and unpitched percussion uses its own percussion clef. To see how each instrument is read, see the notes on each instrument.

In summary

  • Instruments are grouped into families according to how they produce sound. This dictionary distinguishes seven.
  • The scientific classification (Hornbostel–Sachs) looks at the element that produces the sound: chordophones, aerophones, membranophones, idiophones and electrophones.
  • Wood and brass do not refer to the material, but to tradition and to the way the sound is produced.
  • The orchestra rests on the bowed strings; the band, on winds and percussion.
  • In the score, the families usually follow a conventional order (in the orchestra, from the woodwinds down to the strings), which can vary with the work.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the families of musical instruments?
In the symphony orchestra, four large groups are usually distinguished: strings, woodwinds, brass and percussion; in wind bands, winds and percussion dominate. This dictionary uses seven families: it splits the strings into bowed and plucked, the percussion into pitched and unpitched, and adds the keyboard as a practical group.
What is the difference between woodwinds and brass?
It does not depend on the material, but on tradition and on how the sound is produced. In brass, the sound starts when the lips vibrate in a mouthpiece (trumpet, horn, trombone, tuba). In woodwinds, the air passes over a reed or an edge and the notes are changed with keys (flute, oboe, clarinet, bassoon, saxophone). The modern flute and the saxophone are made of metal, but they are counted among the woodwinds.
How are instruments classified in the Hornbostel–Sachs system?
By the element that produces the sound: chordophones (a string, such as the violin or the piano), aerophones (a column of air, such as the flute or the organ), membranophones (a drumhead, such as the snare drum or the timpani), idiophones (the body of the instrument itself, such as cymbals or the xylophone) and electrophones (an electronic signal, such as the synthesizer). It was published in 1914, and the electrophone category was added later.
Which family does the piano belong to?
By its mechanism it is a chordophone (struck strings), because hammers hit the strings. In the orchestra it is treated separately, together with the organ or the celesta, in the practical keyboard family, and its position in the score can vary with the lineup and editing conventions.
