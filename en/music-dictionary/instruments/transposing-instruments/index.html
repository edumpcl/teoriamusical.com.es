Transposing Instruments

A transposing instrument is one whose written note on the score is not the note that actually sounds. If a clarinet player reads a C, the sound that comes out is a B♭; if an alto saxophonist reads that same C, an E♭ sounds. Instruments that sound exactly what is written, such as the piano, the oboe or the trombone, are not transposing instruments.

Here you will find an interactive converter that draws the written and the sounding note on the staff, the full table of transposing instruments with each one's distance, and the rule for transposing a part step by step.

Converter: what is written and what sounds

Choose an instrument and a note. In the first mode you enter the note shown in the part and see what sounds; in the second, you enter the note you want to hear and see what must be written. Concert pitch is the one used when everyone plays together, for example with the piano.

The converter works out the transposition; it does not check whether the note fits the instrument's range.

What "in B♭", "in E♭" or "in F" means

The name of a transposing instrument tells you the note that sounds when the player reads a written C. A clarinet "in B♭" sounds B♭ when it reads C; an alto saxophone "in E♭" sounds E♭; a horn "in F" sounds F. That is why transposition is always measured between what is written and what sounds:

  • In B♭: sounds a major 2nd (a whole tone) lower than written.
  • In A: sounds a minor 3rd lower.
  • In F: sounds a perfect 5th lower.
  • In E♭: the alto saxophone sounds a major 6th lower; the E♭ clarinet (requinto), the other way round, sounds a minor 3rd higher.
  • Octave instruments: they sound one or two octaves above or below what is written, without changing the note name.

The reason is practical. Within one family, like the four saxophones, the same fingering is used and the same music is read: the player switches instrument without relearning anything. On low and high instruments, octave transposition avoids filling the staff with ledger lines. And on horns and trumpets the name comes from the crooks used in the past to change key.

How to transpose a part, step by step

  1. Start from concert pitch: the key and notes as the piano would play them.
  2. Look up how much the instrument transposes in the table above.
  3. Move every note in the opposite direction to the way it sounds: if the instrument sounds lower, the part is written higher. For a B♭ clarinet, every note goes up a major 2nd.
  4. Change the key signature by the same movement. If the band plays in C major, the B♭ clarinet reads in D major.
  5. Check the accidentals: an altered note stays an altered note in the new part, and correct spelling matters as much as the sound.

And the trombone's "transposer"?

If you are searching for the trombone "transposer", what you usually want to know is something else: the trombone is not a transposing instrument. It sounds what is written and reads in bass clef. The trombone's "transposer" is a rotary valve (the F attachment) that adds tubing and lowers the fundamental of each position; it extends the low register and has nothing to do with transposing the score. We explain it in the trombone notes page.

Common mistakes

  • Moving the note the wrong way. If the clarinet sounds a whole tone lower, when writing its part you go up a whole tone, not down.
  • Transposing the notes and forgetting the key signature (or the other way round). Both move together.
  • Thinking that "in B♭" means it is written in B♭ major. It means that, when it reads a C, a B♭ sounds.
  • Treating octave instruments as if they did not transpose. The guitar and the double bass sound an octave lower than written, and the piccolo and the xylophone an octave higher.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a transposing instrument?
It is an instrument whose written note is not the one that sounds. For example, on a B♭ clarinet a written C sounds B♭, a major 2nd lower. The player reads the score as usual, and it is the part that is already written transposed.
Which instruments are transposing instruments?
The most common are the clarinet (in B♭ and in A), the trumpet, the flugelhorn, the saxophones, the horn and the English horn. The piccolo, the xylophone, the contrabassoon, the guitar, the electric bass and the double bass also transpose by an octave. The table on this page lists them all with each one's distance.
Is the trombone a transposing instrument?
No. The tenor trombone sounds what is written and reads in bass clef. What is called the "transposer" on the trombone is the rotary valve or F attachment, which adds tubing to extend the low register and has nothing to do with transposing the score. In the brass band tradition, the euphonium is written in treble clef transposed a major 9th.
Are the bassoon, the oboe and the flute transposing instruments?
No. The flute, the oboe and the bassoon sound what is written. Their relatives do transpose: the piccolo sounds an octave higher, the English horn a perfect 5th lower and the contrabassoon an octave lower.
How do you transpose a B♭ clarinet part?
It is written a major 2nd higher than concert pitch and the key signature also goes up a major 2nd. If the band plays in C major, the clarinet reads in D major; if it plays in F major, it reads in G major.
Why do transposing instruments exist?
For practical reasons: instruments in the same family share fingering and reading (the four saxophones are played the same way), octave instruments avoid filling the staff with ledger lines, and on horns and trumpets the name comes from the crooks used in the past to change key.
