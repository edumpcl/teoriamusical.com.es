Bass Trombone Notes

The bass trombone is the low sibling of the tenor trombone. It has the same slide and the same seven positions, but a wider tube, for a deeper and bigger sound, and it adds two thumb valves that open up the low register. It does not transpose: what is written is what sounds, in bass clef. This page gives its notes, with the slide position and the valves for each one.

Slide, harmonics... and two valves

The basic mechanism is the one of the tenor. The sound starts at the lips, which produce a series of harmonics, and the slide lengthens the tube in seven positions (each a half step lower; UK: semitone) to fill the gaps. From E2 upward, the positions of the bass trombone are the same as those of the tenor.

What is special about the bass trombone is the two valves (rotary valves worked by the left thumb). In the setup used here, B♭/F/G♭/D with independent valves, they work like this:

ValveChanges the instrument to...
1st (F)F (a perfect 4th lower)
2nd (G♭)G♭ (a major 3rd lower)
Both togetherD

Other setups exist, such as dependent systems (where the second valve only works together with the first) and different tunings for the second valve, so a fingering chart must always say which setup it is for.

The valves add tubing. This lets the player reach low notes that the slide alone cannot give, and also offers alternate positions that are more comfortable in many passages. Each valve makes the tube longer, so the slide positions used with the valves are farther apart than the standard ones: the number of the position is a guide for the arm, not an absolute distance.

The low register: filling the gap

Without valves, the trombone goes down to E2 (7th position) and below that only to the pedal tones (B♭1 and lower). In between there is a gap: B1, C2, C♯2, D2 and E♭2 cannot be played. The valves of the bass trombone fill exactly this gap:

NotePosition + valves
E♭2 (D♯2)3rd · F valve
D21st · both valves (D)
C♯22nd · both valves
C23rd · both valves
B14th · both valves

These are the preferred fingerings. There are alternatives (for example, C2 can also be played in a very long 6th position with the F valve). In every case the note is the 2nd harmonic of the series of the valve: for example, with both valves the series starts on D1, and its 2nd harmonic is D2. In the chart below you will see each note with the slide and the valves.

The pedal B♭1 is the lowest note of the chart. It does not need valves: it is the fundamental of the 1st position. With both valves it can also be played in a very long 5th position. Below B♭1 come more pedal tones (A1 in 2nd position, A♭1 in 3rd and so on), which are very low and used only occasionally.

The bass trombone in the band and the orchestra

The bass trombone is the low voice of the trombone section. In the orchestra it completes the trombone trio from below and joins the tuba in the foundation of the brass. In the concert band, which is very present in the Valencian tradition, it adds a low sound with body and definition that supports the whole harmony. Compared with the tenor, its wider tube and larger bell give a rounder and more powerful sound in the low register, and the valves allow it to sustain the lowest notes of the repertoire.

Common mistakes when learning bass trombone notes

  • Thinking there is one position per note. As on the tenor, there are only 7 positions: the same position gives several notes depending on the harmonic. The valves add alternatives, not a new position for every note.
  • Forgetting the valves in the low register. The notes from B1 to E♭2 need a valve. It is impossible to get them with the slide alone.
  • Confusing the position with the real length. With the valves, the physical "4th position" is longer than without them. The position number is a reference for the arm, not an absolute measure.
  • Thinking the valves transpose. They do not change the written note: they only lengthen the tube to reach the low register.

Summary

  • The bass trombone shares the slide and the 7 positions with the tenor, but has a wider tube and two thumb valves.
  • In the B♭/F/G♭/D setup: the 1st valve gives F, the 2nd gives G♭ and both together give D.
  • The valves fill the low gap chromatically (B1, C2, C♯2, D2, E♭2) between the pedal B♭1 and the E2 of the slide alone.
  • It does not transpose (bass clef). It is the low voice of the trombone section.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between the bass trombone and the tenor trombone?
The bass trombone uses the same slide and the same seven positions as the tenor, but it has a wider tube (and a bigger bell) for a deeper and more powerful sound, and it usually adds two thumb valves. Those valves reach low notes that the tenor does not have and fill in the low register chromatically. Both are normally written in bass clef at concert pitch (with tenor clef in high passages) and neither transposes, except the tenor trombone of British brass bands, which is written in treble clef and transposed.
What are the two valves of the bass trombone for?
In the usual B♭/F/G♭/D setup, the first valve changes the instrument to F and the second to G♭, and both together give D. They are used to go down to the low register and, above all, to fill the chromatic gap between the pedal B♭ and the low E (the notes B1, C2, C♯2, D2 and E♭2), which are out of reach with the slide alone.
Does the bass trombone transpose?
No. The bass trombone sounds exactly what is written, and it is read in bass clef. The valves do not change the written note: they only change the length of the tube so that low notes can be reached.
How low can the bass trombone play?
With both valves it plays chromatically down to B1, and the pedal B♭1 is available in first position. Below that are pedal tones (A1, A♭1 and lower), which are very low and used only occasionally. This low register is what defines the bass trombone against the tenor.
