Bassoon Notes

The bassoon is the bass of the double-reed family within the woodwinds. It is pitched in C and does not transpose: the written note is the sounding note. Its deep, warm and sometimes comic voice supports the whole woodwind section. Below you will find its range register by register, the clefs it reads and how its keywork (the German or Heckel system) is organized.

Does the bassoon transpose? Which clef does it read?

No: the written note is the one that sounds. The bassoon usually reads in bass clef. When the music climbs into the upper register it is written in tenor clef (the C clef on the fourth line), and in the highest register sometimes in treble clef, to avoid a pile of ledger lines. Why three clefs? Because the range is more than three octaves. Written in bass clef alone, an E♭5 would need five ledger lines, which is almost unreadable. By changing clef, each register falls inside the staff (UK: stave). That is why bassoonists learn to read fluently in all three.

Range and registers

The standard range runs from the low B♭1 (the B♭ below the bass staff) to the very high E♭5: a little more than three octaves. Advanced players go above E♭5, and with the long bell extension the bottom note goes down to A1. This page divides the range into four registers:

How many notes does the bassoon have?

As on every instrument there are only seven note names (C, D, E, F, G, A and B), but counting accidentals the bassoon produces 42 different pitches in its standard range, from B♭1 to E♭5. The same seven names repeat octave after octave over more than three octaves.

How the sound is made: the double reed and the bocal

Like the oboe, the bassoon sounds with a double reed: two blades of cane tied together that vibrate against each other. The reed is not fitted straight onto the body but onto the bocal (also called the crook), the curved metal tube that connects it to the instrument. The bassoon's tube is about 2.5 meters (8 feet) long, so it is folded back on itself: the air goes down the wing joint to the boot, turns in a U and climbs back up the long joint to the bell, which points up above the player's head.

The German (Heckel) system and its keys

The bassoon is built in two main systems. The German or Heckel system is the standard almost everywhere; the French system (the basson) is now rare. The keywork has a feature that is unique among the woodwinds: the thumbs do a great deal of work. The left thumb alone operates nine or more keys: the whisper key (near the bocal), the high A, C and D keys used in the upper register, several low keys (D, C, B and B♭) and the C♯ key. The right thumb has keys such as the low B♭ key, the big low E key (the "pancake"), an F♯ key and a G♯ key.

Bassoon fingerings

Each note comes from a combination of tone holes and keys. There are six main tone holes (1–3 for the left hand, 4–6 for the right), the thumb keys between the two joints and the little-finger keys at the bottom. For several notes the left index finger covers its hole only halfway (a "half hole").

Choose a note and you will see its fingering, the written note in its real clef (bass, tenor or treble, depending on the register) and a real bassoon sound.

The contrabassoon: the bassoon's low relative

The contrabassoon is the lowest voice of the orchestral woodwinds: the same double-reed principle, but a tube twice as long as the bassoon's. Unlike the bassoon it is an octave transposing instrument: it is written in bass clef like the bassoon and sounds an octave lower than written, down to B♭0, just a half step above the lowest note of the piano. It has its own guide: contrabassoon notes.

Common mistakes when learning bassoon notes

  • Forgetting the half hole. Several notes in the middle and upper register ask you to uncover only half of the first hole of the left hand. If it stays fully covered, the note will not speak or it drops an octave.
  • Not using the thumb keys in the high register. The high A, C and D keys help the upper notes to start cleanly; without them the note tends to crack or gurgle.
  • Neglecting the whisper key. In much of the low and middle register it must be held down: it steadies the low notes and stops the note from jumping to a harmonic. Check a fingering chart for exactly which notes.
  • Getting lost between clefs. A rising passage leaves the bass clef and reappears in tenor clef. It is worth practicing reading both from the start.

In short

  • The bassoon is pitched in C and does not transpose; it reads in bass clef, tenor clef and sometimes treble clef.
  • Its standard range runs from B♭1 to E♭5 (four registers), about 42 pitches.
  • The sound comes from a double reed on the bocal; the 2.5 m tube is folded in a U.
  • It uses the German (Heckel) system: the left thumb handles nine or more keys.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which clef does the bassoon read?
Mainly bass clef. When the music climbs into the high register it is written in tenor clef, and in the very high register treble clef can appear, to avoid too many ledger lines.
Why does the bassoon use three clefs?
Because its range is more than three octaves. If everything were written in bass clef, the high notes would need up to five ledger lines. Switching to tenor clef in the high register and to treble clef in the very high register keeps each register inside the staff and makes reading much easier.
What is the range of the bassoon?
From the low B♭1 to the very high E♭5, a little more than three octaves (about 42 pitches). With the long bell extension it can go down to A1, and professionals go above E♭5.
Is the bassoon a transposing instrument?
No. It is pitched in C and sounds exactly what is written. Its low relative, the contrabassoon, does sound an octave lower than written.
Why is the bassoon so hard to finger?
Because the thumbs do a great deal of work: the left thumb handles nine or more keys (whisper key, high A, C and D keys, several low keys and the C♯ key) and the right thumb several more. In addition, some notes use a half hole on the left index finger.
