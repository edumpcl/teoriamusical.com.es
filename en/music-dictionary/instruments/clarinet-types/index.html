The Clarinet Family

The clarinet is not a single instrument but a whole family: five common members that range from the tiny E♭ clarinet to the giant contrabass clarinet, which can reach down to about 35 Hz (with the standard low E♭ key). They all share the same single reed, the same fingering system and the same way of sounding. What changes is the size and, with it, the pitch. Here are the types of clarinet, what they have in common and what sets them apart, with a comparison chart and a link to the guide for each one.

What is the clarinet family?

The clarinet is a single-reed woodwind: the sound begins when a cane reed vibrates against the mouthpiece. Its most characteristic feature is a cylindrical bore, which has a big acoustic consequence. When the player overblows, the clarinet does not jump an octave like most woodwinds. It jumps a twelfth (an octave plus a fifth). That is why it has the widest range of any woodwind and registers with names of their own (chalumeau, throat, clarion and altissimo) shared by every member of the family.

All clarinets are transposing instruments and are read in treble clef (the contrabass sometimes in bass clef). So when a player changes instrument, they read and finger exactly the same, and only the real pitch that sounds changes.

The five clarinets, from highest to lowest

  • E♭ clarinet (also called the sopranino clarinet; Spanish bands call it requinto): the smallest and highest, with a bright, piercing voice.
  • Clarinet (B♭): the central member, the "violin of the band", which carries the melody and is usually the largest section.
  • Alto clarinet (E♭): the alto voice, the link between the soprano clarinets and the bass.
  • Bass clarinet (B♭): the low voice, deep and velvety, with a curved neck and a metal bell.
  • Contrabass clarinet (B♭): the foundation. It sounds an octave below the bass clarinet, among the lowest wind instruments there are.

What they have in common

What makes this family so recognizable is that its members share the essentials:

  • Essentially the same fingering. Most use the Boehm system with the same finger map, so a clarinetist can play any of them without relearning (as a flutist doubles on piccolo). Some players, especially in Germany and Austria, use the related Oehler system on the B♭ clarinet.
  • Single reed and cylindrical bore, with the register key that gives the jump of a twelfth and the family's four registers.
  • All transpose and are read in treble clef: two are in E♭ (E♭ clarinet and alto) and three in B♭ (clarinet, bass and contrabass).
  • The low instruments add keys for the low E♭ (alto, bass and contrabass; many bass clarinets go lower, to low C) and solve the problem of size with a curved neck and a metal bell.

The clarinet in the band and in the orchestra

The clarinet family is the backbone of the concert band. The B♭ clarinets form the largest section, the equivalent of the violins in an orchestra, and with the E♭ clarinet at the top and the bass and contrabass at the bottom they cover almost the whole range. In the symphony orchestra the usual instruments are the B♭ clarinet (and the A clarinet) plus the bass clarinet. The E♭ clarinet, alto and contrabass appear in particular works and in large ensembles.

A little history

The clarinet was born around 1700 in Nuremberg, thanks to Johann Christoph Denner, who improved the older chalumeau by adding a register key that multiplied its range. During the 18th and 19th centuries the mechanism kept growing, with improvements by Iwan Müller and above all the Boehm system adapted to the clarinet by Klosé and Buffet (around 1840), until the modern clarinet emerged. Meanwhile the family spread upward and downward to cover the whole range, from the E♭ clarinet to the contrabass. In the 20th century it became a mainstay of the concert band.

Other, less common clarinets

Besides the five common ones, the family has rarer relatives that appear in specific repertoire:

  • A clarinet: the twin of the B♭ clarinet, sounding a minor third lower than written. It is standard in the orchestra, where the player carries both and uses whichever suits the key of the piece.
  • Basset horn (corno di bassetto), in F: halfway between the alto and the bass, with extra low notes. Mozart wrote some of his most famous pages for it.
  • A♭ piccolo clarinet: the smallest and highest of all, even above the E♭ clarinet. It is heard mostly in Italian bands and in some wind ensemble and orchestral scores.
  • E♭ contra-alto clarinet: between the bass and the contrabass, another reinforcement of the low register in large ensembles.

They are much less frequent than the five main instruments, but they complete one of the widest families in all of music.

Summary

  • There are five common types of clarinet: E♭ clarinet, clarinet, alto, bass and contrabass, from highest to lowest.
  • All share a single reed, a cylindrical bore (it overblows a twelfth) and essentially the same Boehm fingering.
  • They are transposing instruments read in treble clef: two in E♭ (E♭ clarinet, alto) and three in B♭ (clarinet, bass, contrabass).
  • They are the base of the concert band; in the orchestra the clarinet and the bass clarinet stand out.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many types of clarinet are there?
The five common ones, from highest to lowest, are the E♭ clarinet, the B♭ clarinet, the alto clarinet (E♭), the bass clarinet (B♭) and the contrabass clarinet (B♭). There are also rarer variants, such as the A clarinet, the A♭ piccolo clarinet and the basset horn.
What is the most common clarinet?
The B♭ clarinet, by far. It is the one students learn first and the one that makes up the largest section of the band; in the orchestra it is used alongside the A clarinet. The others are specializations that cover the high and low extremes.
Are they all played the same way?
Essentially yes: almost all use the same Boehm-system finger map (in Germany and Austria the Oehler system is also used) and are read in treble clef. A clarinetist can move from one to another without relearning the fingering, and only the size, the weight and the real pitch change.
Why are some clarinets in E♭ and others in B♭?
It is a historical and practical matter of size and tuning. Since all of them transpose and are written the same way (in treble clef), the player does not notice the difference when reading. The E♭ clarinet and the alto are in E♭, and the clarinet, the bass and the contrabass are in B♭.
