How a Phrase Ends: Strong Beat or Weak Beat

The ending of a musical phrase can fall on a strong beat or on a weak beat of the measure (UK: bar). Depending on where the last note falls, we speak of a strong-beat ending or a weak-beat ending. It is the other side of a phrase, together with its beginning.

Strong-beat ending

The phrase ends with its last note on a strong beat (an accented one). It gives a feeling of firm, final closure, like a full stop. It is the most common ending in marches, hymns and a large part of popular music.

Weak-beat ending

The last note falls on a weak beat, after the accent. The result is a softer, suspended, “in the air” closing, very common in the waltz and in much Romantic-era music.

What matters is not how long the last note lasts, but on which beat it starts. A long or a short note can be a strong or a weak ending; what decides it is whether it begins on the accent or after it.

How to tell whether a phrase ends on a strong or weak beat

  1. Find the last measure of the phrase.
  2. Look at which beat the last note starts on (not how long it lasts).
  3. If it starts on a strong beat, it is a strong-beat ending.
  4. If it starts after the strong beat, it is a weak-beat ending.

If you are unsure which beats are strong or weak, review the rhythmic accent of the measure.

Terminology: masculine and feminine endings

In English-language theory these endings have traditionally been called masculine (on a strong beat) and feminine (on a weak beat). The terms are borrowed from poetry and are still widely used, for example in the study of phrase rhythm and of Classical-era melodies. They are only labels: they say nothing musical about the sound itself. Many teachers prefer the plain descriptions strong-beat ending and weak-beat ending, which are clearer and are used on this site. Spanish-language texts also use the technical terms íctico (ending on the ictus, the strong beat) and posíctico (ending after the ictus); these are rare in English.

Rhythmic ending and cadence are not the same

This ending describes the rhythm (on which beat the last note falls). It must not be confused with the cadence, which is the harmonic ending: the chord progression (V–I, IV–I…) that closes the phrase. A phrase has both at once: a rhythmic ending, strong or weak, and a harmonic ending, its cadence.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a strong-beat and a weak-beat ending?
In a strong-beat ending the last note falls on the accent of the measure and gives a firm conclusion. In a weak-beat ending the last note falls after the accent and the closing feels softer or suspended.
What are masculine and feminine endings?
They are the traditional names: a masculine ending falls on a strong beat and a feminine ending on a weak beat. They are borrowed from poetry and say nothing about the sound itself, so describing the ending as strong-beat or weak-beat is clearer.
Does a long last note make a strong ending?
No. What decides the type of ending is the beat on which the last note starts, not how long it lasts.
Is the end of a phrase the same as a cadence?
No. The ending discussed here is rhythmic (on which beat the last note falls). The cadence is the harmonic ending: the chord progression that closes the phrase. A phrase has both at once.
