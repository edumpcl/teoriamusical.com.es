How a Phrase Begins: Downbeat, Pickup or Off-Beat Start

In music theory, the beginning of a phrase can be of three types, depending on where its first note falls in relation to the first strong beat of the measure (UK: bar). Telling them apart helps you phrase well, prepare an entrance when conducting or singing, and analyze a piece correctly.

The names thetic, anacrusic and acephalous come from the Spanish-language analysis tradition. English-language books usually just say that a phrase starts on the downbeat, with a pickup (UK: upbeat; the technical word is anacrusis), or on an off-beat after a rest. Both sets of names are given here.

  • Thetic (starts on the downbeat): the first note falls on the strong beat (beat 1 of the measure).
  • Anacrusic (starts with a pickup): the phrase begins before the strong beat, with an anacrusis.
  • Acephalous (starts after a rest on the downbeat): the strong beat is silent and the music enters just after it.

Thetic beginning (on the downbeat)

A thetic beginning starts on beat 1 of the measure, right on the strong beat. It is the most common type and sounds firm from the first note. The first measure is complete.

Anacrusic beginning (with a pickup)

An anacrusic beginning starts on a weak part of the measure, before the first strong beat, with one or more notes called an anacrusis (US: pickup). These notes create momentum toward the first strong beat. The first measure is incomplete, and it is completed by the last measure of the phrase: together they add up to one full measure.

Very many melodies begin with a pickup. Well-known examples are Happy Birthday and Beethoven’s Für Elise.

Acephalous beginning (off-beat start after a rest)

“Acephalous” means headless: this beginning leaves the first strong beat (or part of it) silent and the music enters just after it, on a weak part. The initial rest may be written (the measure looks complete, starting with a rest) or not (the measure looks incomplete). What separates it from a pickup is when the music enters. In the Spanish tradition, in an acephalous beginning the music enters after the strong beat but before the middle of the measure; in an anacrusis it enters at the middle of the measure or later. Not every textbook draws the line in exactly this place, so use it as a working rule.

The opening of Beethoven’s Fifth Symphony is a classic example: an eighth rest (UK: quaver rest) on the downbeat, then the three short notes.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an anacrusis (pickup)?
An anacrusis is the note or notes that come before the first strong beat of the measure, on a weak beat. They give momentum toward the accent and create an anacrusic beginning. Songs such as Happy Birthday begin with a pickup.
What is the difference between an anacrusic and an acephalous beginning?
It depends on when the music enters. In an anacrusic beginning it enters at the middle of the measure or later, with real notes before the bar line (the anacrusis) and an incomplete first measure. In an acephalous beginning it enters after the strong beat but before the middle of the measure: the first beat, or part of it, is silent, whether or not the rest is written.
How is the measure of a pickup completed?
The incomplete measure of the pickup is completed by the last measure of the phrase: the beats of the pickup plus the beats of the final measure add up to one full measure.
What is a thetic beginning?
It is a beginning on the first beat of the measure, on the strong beat. The first measure is complete, and this is the most common type of beginning.
