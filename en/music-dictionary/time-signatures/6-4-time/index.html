6/4 Time Signature

6/4 time is a compound meter with two beats: the six quarter notes (UK: crotchets) group in threes to form two pulses, each equal to a dotted half note (UK: dotted minim). It is the "big brother" of 6/8: the same structure, but with the quarter note as the subdivision instead of the eighth note.

What does 6/4 mean?

In the time signature, the top number 6 says that six quarter notes fit in the measure (UK: bar) and the bottom number 4 says the reference note value is the quarter note. Those six quarter notes group into two sets of three, so 6/4 is normally counted in two (although at very slow tempos it can be felt in six).

How to count 6/4

Each of the two beats divides into three quarter notes (ternary subdivision). The six quarter notes group 3+3, with the strong accent on the first note of each group:

Where is 6/4 used?

  • Renaissance and Baroque music: frequent in broad passages of a solemn character.
  • Modern music: it appears in passages that look for a wide, swaying triple feel.

6/4 compared with 6/8 and 3/2

6/4 and 6/8 have the same structure: two compound beats grouped 3+3. Only the beat unit changes (a dotted half note in 6/4, a dotted quarter note in 6/8). 3/2, on the other hand, also holds six quarter notes, but grouped 2+2+2 (three simple beats), not 3+3.

6/4 and the hemiola (3 against 2)

The fact that 6/4 and 3/2 hold the same six quarter notes, grouped 3+3 in one and 2+2+2 in the other, is the basis of an important rhythmic device, the hemiola (from the Greek hēmiólios, "one and a half," the 3:2 ratio).

A hemiola happens when, within a measure of 6/4 (or across two measures of 3/4), the music re-accents the six quarter notes as three groups of two instead of two groups of three. For a moment the ear moves from feeling "two" (6/4) to feeling "three" (3/2), without any change in the time signature:

The hemiola is typical of Baroque cadences (Bach and Handel use it constantly before the final chord), of the French courante, and of Spanish and Latin American music: the 6/8 ↔ 3/4 sway of the jota, the guajira or the huapango, known in Spanish tradition as the sesquialtera, is the same game of 3 against 2.

How to practice 6/4

Practice the two ternary beats with a steady pulse on the online metronome, and check the time signature of a score with the time signature analyzer.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is 6/4 time?
6/4 is a compound meter with two beats. The six quarter notes group in threes (3+3), so it is counted in two pulses, each equal to a dotted half note.
Is 6/4 simple or compound?
6/4 is normally treated as a compound meter with two beats, with the dotted half note as the beat unit. At very slow tempos it can also be felt in six.
What is the difference between 6/4 and 6/8?
They have the same structure (two compound beats, grouped 3+3). Only the beat unit changes: a dotted half note in 6/4 and a dotted quarter note in 6/8.
What is the difference between 6/4 and 3/2?
Both hold six quarter notes, but 6/4 groups them 3+3 (two compound beats) and 3/2 groups them 2+2+2 (three simple beats).
