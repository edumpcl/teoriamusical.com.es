Musical Cadences

A cadence is the harmonic formula that closes or marks off a musical phrase: a group of chords that gives a feeling of rest, suspension or surprise. In harmony, cadences are the equivalent of punctuation marks in language. Almost all of them are defined by their last two chords and their relation to the key. All the examples are in C major.

Types of cadence

Cadence names differ between American and British usage, so both are given. The table uses the American names; the British equivalents follow in the next paragraph.

CadenceChordsCharacter
Perfect authentic (PAC)V → I (both in root position, tonic in the top voice)Strongest conclusion
Imperfect authentic (IAC)V → I (with an inversion, or without the tonic on top)Weaker conclusion
PlagalIV → IGentle rest (“Amen”)
DeceptiveV → viSurprise: does not conclude
Half cadence… → VSuspensive (a “comma”)

UK names. The British “perfect cadence” is any V–I (or V7–I) in the root position, roughly what Americans call an authentic cadence. The British “imperfect cadence” is what Americans call a half cadence (a phrase that stops on V). The British “interrupted cadence” is the American deceptive cadence. “Plagal” is the same in both.

Authentic cadence (V – I)

This is the conclusive cadence par excellence: the dominant chord (V or V7) resolves to the tonic (I). The tritone inside the dominant “demands” that resolution. Depending on how it is presented, there are two kinds:

  • Perfect authentic: V and I in root position and with the tonic in the highest voice of the final chord. It gives the strongest feeling of conclusion and is the one that ends a piece.
  • Imperfect authentic: still V–I, but it fails one of those conditions (a chord is inverted, or the top voice of the I chord is the 3rd or 5th instead of the tonic). It concludes, but less firmly.

Plagal cadence (IV – I)

It connects the subdominant (IV) with the tonic (I). It sounds softer, more solemn and more “old-fashioned” than the authentic cadence, because it does not contain the tension of the tritone. It is the famous “Amen” cadence that ends many hymns.

Deceptive cadence (V – vi)

The dominant (V), which seems about to resolve to the tonic, resolves instead to the vi chord (the relative minor). The ear expects rest and gets a surprise: the phrase does not conclude and the music continues. In British usage it is called the interrupted cadence.

Half cadence (… – V)

This is a suspensive cadence: the phrase ends on the dominant (V), usually preceded by I, ii or IV, instead of resolving to the tonic. It does not close: it leaves a feeling of a question, a “musical comma” that asks for continuation. It is very common at the end of the first phrase of a theme, before the answer. (In British usage it is the imperfect cadence.)

Pause on the subdominant (… – IV)

Some Spanish-language textbooks also teach a half cadence that ends on the subdominant (IV). It is equally suspensive, but its effect is softer and more relaxed than that of the half cadence on V, because the subdominant does not create the tension characteristic of V. In English-language theory the term “half cadence” normally means ending on V, so this ending is usually just described as a phrase that stops on IV.

Cadences and scale degrees

All cadences are built with chords raised on the degrees of the scale: the tonic (I), the subdominant (IV) and above all the dominant (V), the three tonal degrees that support the harmony (this is what is studied in harmonic functions: tonic, subdominant and dominant). To go deeper into the chords involved, see seventh chords and triads.

To practice, there is an exercise on recognizing the type of cadence from its last two chords, written at the piano as in a four-part chorale.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a musical cadence?
A cadence is the harmonic formula that closes or marks off a musical phrase, normally with two or more chords that give a feeling of rest, suspension or surprise. In harmony it is the equivalent of punctuation marks in language.
How many types of cadence are there?
The main ones are: the authentic cadence (V–I), which can be perfect or imperfect; the plagal cadence (IV–I); the deceptive cadence (V–vi); and the half cadence (suspensive), which ends on V.
What is the difference between a perfect and an imperfect authentic cadence?
A perfect authentic cadence meets three conditions: V and I in root position and the tonic in the highest voice of the final chord; it gives the strongest feeling of conclusion. An imperfect authentic cadence fails one of these (an inverted chord, or the 3rd or 5th in the top voice of the I chord), so its conclusion is weaker.
What is a plagal cadence?
The plagal cadence is the progression IV–I (subdominant to tonic). It sounds softer and older than the authentic cadence; it is the “Amen” cadence that closes many hymns.
What is a half cadence?
A half cadence is a suspensive cadence that does not resolve to the tonic: the phrase stops on the dominant (V). It leaves a feeling of a question or “musical comma” that asks for continuation. In British usage it is called an imperfect cadence.
What are the UK names for cadences?
The British “perfect cadence” is V–I (American: authentic cadence), the “imperfect cadence” is the American half cadence, and the “interrupted cadence” is the American deceptive cadence. “Plagal cadence” is the same in both.
