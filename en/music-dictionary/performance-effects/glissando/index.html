Glissando

A glissando (abbreviated gliss.) is an effect in which the sound slides from one note to another. Depending on the instrument, it can be a continuous slide through all the pitches in between (voice, violin, slide trombone) or a very fast run through the notes of a scale (piano, harp), where you hear the individual notes. It is written with a line joining the two notes.

How it is written

A glissando is shown by a straight line (sometimes wavy) that goes from the first notehead to the second, often with the abbreviation gliss. above it. The exact notation can vary with the instrument, the edition and the effect wanted. The starting note and the arrival note are written; the pitches in between are not. If the line goes up, the glissando is ascending; if it goes down, it is descending.

Sometimes only the starting note is written, with a line that rises or falls and no arrival note. This is a glissando of indefinite pitch: the performer decides how far it goes.

Types of glissando

Depending on the instrument, a glissando can be continuous or stepped:

TypeWhat it is likeInstruments
ContinuousThe pitch changes smoothly between the starting note and the arrival note.Voice, bowed strings (the finger slides along the string), the slide trombone, pedal timpani and slide guitar. The clarinet can do it by combining special fingerings and the embouchure, especially in certain registers.
SteppedFixed pitches are played very fast, like the keys of a piano: you hear the successive notes, but so close together that it sounds like a slide.Piano, harp and mallet percussion (xylophone, marimba, vibraphone), drawing the mallets across the bars.

The piano glissando

On the piano, it is played by quickly sliding one or more fingers (often the fingernail) across the keys, with a technique adapted to the direction of the movement so it does not hurt. On the white keys you hear the C major scale, and on the black keys the F♯ major pentatonic scale (F♯, G♯, A♯, C♯, D♯). Glissando and portamento

The glissando and the portamento are related effects, and in some contexts their names are used almost interchangeably. As a rough guide, a glissando usually marks a run between two pitches, often with a written instruction in the score; a portamento is an expressive link between two notes, especially in singing and on bowed strings, where the pitch slides toward the target note and which is often decided by the performer. The difference is not absolute: it depends on the instrument and the tradition. Famous examples

  • Rhapsody in Blue (1924), by George Gershwin: the piece opens with a clarinet trill and a rising 17-note scale that the clarinetist turns into a glissando, one of the best-known in 20th-century music. The most repeated story says it began in a rehearsal, when clarinetist Ross Gorman slid up the top of the scale as a joke and Gershwin decided to keep it.
  • Beethoven's "Waldstein" Sonata (Op. 53): in the coda of the last movement there are glissandos in octaves.
  • The trombone uses it in the orchestra, the wind band and jazz for rising and falling effects; it is the brass instrument that does it most naturally.

How to practice a glissando with your voice

Singing a glissando is a good exercise in pitch control: you have to change pitch continuously and at a steady speed, and arrive on the target note on time. It is practiced with a "siren": a long vowel ("oo" or "oh") that rises and falls without jumps. To turn it into a game, try javelin throw, where a lane guides you and measures how long you stay on the ideal path.

To know how far apart the two notes are, review musical intervals. The glissando is one of the performance effects, along with portamento, the arpeggiated chord, vibrato and tremolo. For other signs that change how a note sounds, see musical ornaments and articulation.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a glissando?

Depending on the instrument, it can be a continuous slide through all the pitches in between, or a very fast run through the notes of a scale, as on the piano. It is abbreviated gliss. and written with a line joining the two notes.
How is a glissando written in a score?
With a straight line, or sometimes a wavy one, from the first note to the second, often with the abbreviation gliss. above it. If the line goes up, the glissando is ascending; if it goes down, descending. The notation can vary with the instrument and the edition.
What is the difference between glissando and portamento?

Which instruments can play a continuous glissando?
