Augmented Chord

The augmented chord is a triad with a tense, unstable and mysterious sound. It is built with a major 3rd and an augmented 5th above the root, which means two major thirds stacked. The augmented 5th (a half step (UK: semitone) wider than a perfect 5th) takes away the sense of rest and makes the chord "ask" to resolve. It is one of the four types of triad, together with the major, the minor and the diminished triads.

How to build an augmented chord

Stack two major thirds: one on the root and another on top of it. Between the lowest and the highest note you get an augmented 5th. With C as the root:

NoteChord memberInterval above the root
CRoot—
EThirdMajor 3rd (2 whole steps; UK: tones)
G♯FifthAugmented 5th (4 whole steps)

Compared with the major triad (C–E–G), the augmented chord is identical except that the fifth is raised a half step (G♯ instead of G). That small change completely transforms its character.

Augmented chord symbol

In lead-sheet chord symbols the augmented chord is written with a "+" sign or the abbreviation "aug"; a "(♯5)" or "(5+)" is also seen. So the augmented chord on C is written C+, Caug or C(♯5). The table below gives the symbols for all twelve.

All augmented chords are built the same way: root + major 3rd + augmented 5th. Here is the reference table with all twelve, their notes and their symbols:

Notice that the augmented 5th forces some accidentals that other chords do not need: in B augmented you must use a double sharp (F double sharp), because the note has to keep the letter F for the interval to be a 5th and not a 6th.

Where does the augmented chord appear?

Unlike major and minor chords, the augmented chord does not occur naturally in the major scale. It appears on the third degree of the harmonic minor scale (and of the ascending melodic minor), where the raised leading tone creates the augmented 5th. In practice it is used mostly as a color or passing chord: its characteristic tension resolves well by moving the augmented 5th up to the 6th. To see how each degree is named, see the scale degrees.

Because it is symmetrical (it divides the octave into three equal major thirds), the augmented chord has only four different sounds: every augmented chord is enharmonically equal to two others. In the table above they group like this: C+ = E+ = A♭+; D♭+ = F+ = A+; D+ = G♭+ = B♭+; E♭+ = G+ = B+. They sound the same, but each is spelled differently depending on the key.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an augmented chord?
An augmented chord is a triad made of a major 3rd and an augmented 5th above the root. On C the notes are C–E–G♯. It is unstable and tense, and it asks for resolution.
How do you build an augmented chord?
Stack two major thirds: one on the root and one on top. On C: C to E is a major 3rd, E to G♯ is another major 3rd, and C to G♯ is an augmented 5th (a half step larger than a perfect 5th).
How is an augmented chord written as a symbol?
The root letter followed by "+" or "aug": C+ or Caug for C augmented. It may also be written C(♯5).
Where does the augmented chord appear?
Naturally on the third degree of the harmonic minor scale (and of the ascending melodic minor). It is also used as a passing or color chord, because its tension tends to resolve by opening the augmented 5th up to a 6th.
