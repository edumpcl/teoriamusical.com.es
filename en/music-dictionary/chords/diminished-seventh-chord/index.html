Diminished Seventh Chord

The diminished seventh chord is a four-note chord built on the seventh degree (the leading tone; UK: leading note) of the minor key, or of the major key with a lowered sixth degree. It is made of three minor thirds stacked on top of each other. It is the most tense of the seventh chords. For background, see the pages on scale degrees and musical intervals.

Construction and unique properties

In the harmonic minor scale, the raised seventh degree (the leading tone) gives the diminished seventh chord. In A harmonic minor:

  • Root: G♯ (degree VII, the leading tone)
  • Minor 3rd: B (3 half steps; UK: semitones)
  • Diminished 5th: D (6 half steps)
  • Diminished 7th: F (9 half steps)

The most unusual property of this chord is that all its notes are a minor third apart (exactly 3 half steps each). This makes the chord symmetrical: all four of its inversions have the same structure of intervals. In practice, a diminished seventh chord can be spelled in four ways, because the same four pitches can be named from any of them as the root: G♯°7, B°7, D°7 and F°7 contain the same sounds (with enharmonic respelling of the notes).

This ambiguity makes it one of the most common chords for modulating suddenly to distant keys: from any of its four notes it can be resolved as the leading-tone chord of a different key.

Comparison with the half-diminished chord

The half-diminished chord is the leading-tone seventh chord: it starts from the same diminished triad, but its seventh is minor instead of diminished, so it appears in major and is not symmetrical. The table sums up the difference:

Root position

The examples below use C harmonic minor, where the chord is B–D–F–A♭. It is built with a minor 3rd, a diminished 5th and a diminished 7th above the root.

Figured bass

The figured bass is a slashed 7 and a slashed 5: the slashed 7 says that the seventh is diminished and the slashed 5 says that the fifth is diminished. It is the same in every key.

First inversion

The third is in the bass. Above the bass the chord has a minor 3rd, a diminished 5th and a major 6th.

Figured bass

The figured bass is +6 and a slashed 5: the +6 says that the leading tone is a sixth above the bass and the slashed 5 says that the fifth is diminished. It is the same in every key.

Second inversion

The fifth is in the bass. Above the bass the chord has a minor 3rd, an augmented 4th and a major 6th.

Figured bass

The figured bass is +4 and the corresponding accidental on the 3: the +4 says that the fourth is the leading tone, and the 3 with whatever accidental the key requires gives the third. The figured bass varies with the key.

Third inversion

The seventh is in the bass. Above the bass the chord has an augmented 2nd, an augmented 4th and a major 6th.

Figured bass

The figured bass is +2: the +2 says that the second is the leading tone. It is the same in every key.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a diminished seventh chord?
It is a four-note chord made of three stacked minor thirds: a diminished triad plus a diminished 7th. It is built on the seventh degree of the minor key, or of the major key with a lowered sixth degree.
How does it differ from the leading-tone (half-diminished) seventh chord?
The diminished seventh has a diminished 7th (a major 6th spelled as a 7th, 9 half steps above the root), while the half-diminished has a minor 7th (10 half steps). As a result the diminished seventh is completely symmetrical: its four notes are spread in equal minor thirds.
Why does the diminished seventh chord have so many enharmonic spellings?
Because it is made of equal minor thirds, its four positions (root position and three inversions) contain the same intervals, so each inversion sounds like the root position of another diminished seventh. C♯°7 and E°7, for example, sound exactly the same.
How many inversions does it have?
Three, like every four-note chord. In the first inversion the third is in the bass, in the second the fifth, and in the third the seventh.
What is the diminished seventh chord used for?
Mainly as a substitute for the dominant (V7), resolving to the tonic with great tension, especially in minor-key cadences. It is also used as a chromatic passing chord between two diatonic chords, and its symmetry makes it very useful for modulating to distant keys.
