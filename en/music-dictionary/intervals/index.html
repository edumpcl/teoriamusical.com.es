Intervals

Musical intervals are the distance in pitch between two notes, measured in whole steps (UK: tones) and half steps (UK: semitones). They are the basic unit of harmony: scales, chords and melodies are built from them. This section gathers all the concepts: definition, classification by number and quality, consonance and dissonance, inversion, widening and narrowing.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a musical interval?
A musical interval is the distance in pitch between two notes, measured in whole steps and half steps. It is the basic unit of harmony: scales, chords and melodies are built from intervals.
How are intervals classified?
Intervals are classified by number (how many letter names they span: 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 5th, 6th, 7th or octave) and by quality (perfect, major, minor, augmented or diminished). They are also divided into simple and compound intervals.
What is the difference between a harmonic and a melodic interval?
A harmonic interval is formed when the two notes sound at the same time. A melodic interval occurs when the notes sound one after the other, forming a sequence.
How do you invert an interval?
To invert an interval, move the lower note up an octave (or the upper note down an octave). The number changes: an interval and its inversion always add up to 9. The quality changes too: major becomes minor, augmented becomes diminished, and perfect stays perfect.
What are consonant and dissonant intervals?
Consonant intervals sound stable and do not need to resolve: unison, octave, perfect 5th, and major and minor 3rds and 6ths. Dissonant intervals create tension and ask to resolve: 2nds, 7ths and the tritone (augmented 4th / diminished 5th). The perfect 4th is a special case: consonant in a melody and often treated as dissonant in harmony.
