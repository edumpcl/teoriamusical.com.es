Diatonic and Chromatic Half Steps

A diatonic half step (UK: diatonic semitone) is formed between two notes with different letter names, such as E–F or B–C. A chromatic half step is formed between two notes with the same letter name and a different accidental, such as C–C♯. Both measure exactly the same, one half step, but they are written and they work differently. This distinction is one of the keys to music notation and to the study of whole steps and half steps: the diatonic half step is written as a minor 2nd and the chromatic half step as an augmented unison.

Type Notes Examples Written interval
Diatonic Different letter names E–F, B–C, A♯–B, D♯–E minor 2nd
Chromatic Same letter name C–C♯, G♯–G, B–B♯, E–E♭ Augmented unison

In equal temperament both types measure exactly the same (100 cents, one half step). The difference lies only in spelling and in harmonic function.

What is a diatonic half step?

A diatonic half step is one in which the letter names of the two notes are different, for example E–F, B–C or A♯–B, always keeping the distance of one half step. Because it uses two different letter names, it is written as a minor 2nd, and it is the half step that occurs naturally within scales.

How to tell the two types apart

The rule is simple: look at the letter names.

  • If the two notes have different letter names (C and D♭, E and F, A♯ and B) → diatonic. It is written as a minor 2nd.
  • If the two notes have the same letter name (C and C♯, G and G♭) → chromatic. It is written as an augmented unison.

In scales and most melodies, the half steps between consecutive scale degrees are diatonic: the notes always have different letter names. Chromatic half steps appear when a note is altered in passing, as in a chromatic passage, where the direction of the line usually decides the spelling (C–C♯ going up, D–D♭ going down). Writing C–C♯ where the key and the context call for C–D♭ is a spelling error, even though the notes sound the same on the piano.

Where diatonic half steps appear in scales

ScaleDiatonic half stepsBetween scale degrees
C majorE–F, B–C3–4 and 7–8
A natural minorB–C, E–F2–3 and 5–6
A harmonic minorB–C, E–F, G♯–A2–3, 5–6 and 7–8
A melodic minor (ascending)B–C, G♯–A2–3 and 7–8

In all these cases, the half steps within the scale are diatonic: between notes with different letter names.

The chromatic half step is closely related to the idea of enharmonic notes: C and C♯ share a letter name, but C♯ sounds the same as D♭, which has a different letter name.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a diatonic half step?
A diatonic half step is the distance of a half step between two notes with different letter names, such as E–F or B–C. It is the natural half step that appears between the 3rd and 4th degrees and between the 7th and 8th degrees of the major scale.
What is a chromatic half step?
A chromatic half step is the distance of a half step between two notes with the same letter name and a different accidental, such as C–C♯ or F–F♯. It alters a note without changing its scale degree.
What is the difference between a diatonic and a chromatic half step?
A diatonic half step is formed between two notes with different letter names, such as E and F. A chromatic half step is formed between two notes with the same letter name and a different accidental, such as C and C sharp.
Do a diatonic and a chromatic half step sound the same?
Yes. In equal temperament both measure exactly the same. The difference is in the spelling: the diatonic half step uses two different letter names and the chromatic half step uses the same letter name with a different accidental.
What is an example of a diatonic half step?
The most common examples are E–F and B–C among the natural notes. A♯–B and D♯–E are also diatonic half steps, because they use two different letter names.
Where do diatonic half steps fall in the major scale?
In the major scale, the diatonic half steps always fall between the 3rd and 4th degrees (E–F in C major) and between the 7th and 8th degrees (B–C in C major). These two fixed positions give the major scale its characteristic sound.
Why does it matter to tell diatonic and chromatic half steps apart?
Because tonal notation uses a different letter name for each scale degree: a diatonic half step (E–F) and a chromatic one (E–E♯) sound the same but are written differently. Using the wrong type produces spelling errors and makes the music harder to read at sight.
