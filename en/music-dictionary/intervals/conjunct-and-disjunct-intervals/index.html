Conjunct and Disjunct Intervals

Depending on the distance between its two notes, an interval is either conjunct or disjunct. This is a useful way to describe how a melody moves: does it go note by note (by step) or by leaps?

Conjunct interval

A conjunct interval (moving "by conjunct degrees", or by step) joins two neighboring notes of the scale, that is, a 2nd (major or minor). The two notes are next to each other, with no note between them. For example, from C to D:

Disjunct interval

A disjunct interval (a leap) joins two notes that are not neighbors: the melody skips one or more notes in between. It is any interval of a 3rd or larger. For example, from C to G (a 5th):

The boundary is simple: only the 2nd is conjunct; everything else (3rd, 4th, 5th, 6th…) is disjunct.

Stepwise and leaping motion in a melody

These two kinds of interval describe melodic motion. A melody that moves by steps (from 2nd to 2nd) sounds smooth, gradual and singable. For example, C–D–E–F:

A melody that moves by leaps sounds more angular and energetic. It is typical of arpeggios and instrumental themes. For example, C–G–E–C:

Most melodies mix both: stepwise stretches give flow, and an occasional leap adds interest and expression.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a conjunct interval?
A conjunct interval is the interval between two neighboring notes of the scale, that is, a 2nd (major or minor), for example from C to D. Conjunct motion moves note by note, without leaps.
What is a disjunct interval?
A disjunct interval separates two notes that are not neighbors, that is, a leap of a 3rd or more, for example from C to G. Disjunct motion moves by leaps and skips the notes in between.
What is the difference between conjunct and disjunct?
A conjunct interval joins neighboring notes (a 2nd) in a smooth, stepwise way; a disjunct interval joins notes separated by a leap (a 3rd or more). The difference is the distance: adjacent notes in conjunct motion, separated notes in disjunct motion.
Is a 3rd conjunct or disjunct?
A 3rd is disjunct, because one note between the two is skipped. Only the 2nd (from one scale degree to the next) is conjunct; every interval from the 3rd upward is disjunct.
