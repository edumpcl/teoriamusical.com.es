Rhythmic Dictation with Dotted Half Notes and Sixteenth Notes

Listen to a rhythm of two measures (UK: bars) in 3/4 or 4/4, played on the note G, and write it with cards for dotted half notes (UK: dotted minims), half notes (UK: minims), quarter notes (UK: crotchets), groups of eighth notes (UK: quavers) and of sixteenth notes (UK: semiquavers) and their rests. The eighth-note and sixteenth-note cards are single notes: for a group, place them in a row and they are joined by beams. You place the bar lines yourself: a bar line at the end of the first measure and a final double bar line.

How to use the exercise: press Start and then Listen. As soon as the audio begins, the cards become active, and each card is written after the previous one. You place the bar lines yourself: the bar line when you finish the first measure and the final double bar line at the end. Nothing stops you from putting more beats in a measure than fit, or fewer: you have to count them yourself, as in a classroom dictation. Undo removes the last item and Clear all starts over. Once the double bar line is in place, press Check. You can listen up to three times and write while it plays.

How to Write a Rhythmic Dictation with Dotted Half Notes and Sixteenth Notes

  1. Settle the pulse first. Sixteenth notes are fast, so take the count-in measure seriously: feel the beats ("1, 2, 3, 4") and keep tapping your foot. Everything you hear is placed against that steady beat.
  2. Identify the meter and count its beats. The time signature, 3/4 or 4/4, is announced before you listen: count its beats from the count-in measure and start again at 1 in every measure, even if the first beat is silent.
  3. Count the notes in each beat. One note is a quarter note, two are eighth notes and four are sixteenth notes. If a note keeps sounding into the following beats, it is a longer value, and if nothing sounds, it is a rest.
  4. Listen in parts. Both measures play each time, but you do not have to catch everything at once: on the first listen follow the pulse and the first measure; on the second, the second measure; on the third, check the whole dictation.

The usual mistakes are mixing up eighth notes and sixteenth notes and rushing through the sixteenths, so that the next beat seems to arrive early. Four sixteenth notes last as long as two eighth notes or one quarter note. Say "ti-ri-ti-ri" for sixteenths, "ti-ti" for eighths and "ta" for a quarter note while you tap the beat, and every syllable lands where it should.

What the Dictations Are Like

Each dictation is generated on the spot: two measures in 3/4 or 4/4 with dotted half notes, half notes, quarter notes, groups of eighth notes and of sixteenth notes and their rests, all on the note G on the piano at a tempo between 60 and 72 quarter notes per minute, a little slower because of the sixteenth notes. Sixteenth notes always come in fours, joined by a double beam and filling one beat, and eighth notes in pairs: in this group there is never a loose one (a lone eighth note appears only in the groups with an eighth rest). Every dictation has at least one group of four sixteenth notes and a dotted half note or its rest. A measure of metronome clicks plays first at the same tempo, with a stronger first beat, so you know the speed and where the measure starts. A dictation can begin with a rest. Up to three beats of rest can follow one another, the length of a dotted half rest. A dotted half note lasts three beats and does not fit in a measure of 2/4, so there are no dictations in 2/4.

Each note sounds for almost its full value, so four sixteenth notes are heard as four very short notes splitting the beat into four equal parts, twice as fast as eighth notes. Each test has 10 dictations: 5 in 3/4 and 5 in 4/4, with none repeated.

Frequently asked questions

How is the dictation checked?
It counts as correct only if everything is right: the notes and rests, the bar line and the number of beats in each measure. If you miss, you are told which measure has too many or too few beats; if the total length is right, the parts that sound different are shown in red in what you wrote, and below it you see the rhythm that played so you can listen again while reading it.
Why do I have to place the bar lines myself?
Because in a written dictation you place them yourself, and it is how you show that you counted the beats of each measure correctly. If a measure has too many or too few beats, the check tells you how many it has and how many fit, and whether the bar line is missing. Without the final double bar line the exercise is not checked: it is your signal that you have finished.
Why are the cards single sixteenth notes?
So that you build the group yourself: four sixteenth notes in a row that start on a beat are joined by a double beam and fill that beat (two eighth notes, by a single beam). If you place fewer, they are shown loose and, when checked, that beat will not sound like the dictation, because here sixteenth notes always come in fours.
What is the measure of clicks at the beginning for?
It tells you the speed and where the measure begins: before each audio, a metronome click sounds on every beat, stronger on the first, at the dictation's tempo. Count with it and keep counting silently while the rhythm plays, including the rests.
Why are there no dictations in 2/4?
Because a dotted half note lasts three beats and does not fit in a measure of two beats. It could be split across two measures with a tie, but these dictations contain no ties.

If you find it hard, start with which rhythm is playing? or which audio is it?. The next group is whole notes and sixteenth notes. Every group is listed in the rhythmic dictation index.
