Identify Triads in Root Position

A triad is made of three notes stacked in thirds: the root, the third and the fifth. The quality of those thirds gives four different types of chord. In root position the root is the lowest note, so the chord looks like three notes on neighboring lines or neighboring spaces.

1. The four types of triad

TypeThirdFifthExample
MajorMajor 3rd (4 half steps)Perfect 5th (7)C – E – G
Minorminor 3rd (3 half steps)Perfect 5th (7)D – F – A
Diminishedminor 3rd (3 half steps)Diminished 5th (6)B – D – F
AugmentedMajor 3rd (4 half steps)Augmented 5th (8)C – E – G♯

The names of the types are also written as "perfect major" and "perfect minor" in some textbooks, and the diminished and augmented triads are named after their fifth.

2. How to analyze a triad

Quick method:

  1. Find the lowest note (the root).
  2. Count the half steps (UK: semitones) up to the middle note, the third: 4 = major, 3 = minor.
  3. Count the half steps up to the top note, the fifth: 7 = perfect, 6 = diminished, 8 = augmented.
  4. Combine: major third + perfect fifth = major; minor third + perfect fifth = minor; minor third + diminished fifth = diminished; major third + augmented fifth = augmented.

3. Interactive exercise

A three-note chord appears on the staff. Your task is to identify its type by choosing one of the four options.

Next: identify triads in first inversion, or practice the opposite skill and build triads in root position.

If you practice these chords on an instrument, the online metronome helps you build speed gradually: start slowly (50-60 BPM) and speed up a little at a time. The online tuner lets you check that every note of the chord is in tune.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the root position of a chord?
Root position means that the lowest note of the chord (the bass) is the root, the note that gives the chord its name. In a C major chord in root position, C is in the bass and the upper notes are E and G.
How do you recognize a root-position triad on the staff?
The bass is the root, and the three notes form a stack of thirds with no inversion: root, third, fifth from the bottom up. On the staff the notes sit on three adjacent lines or three adjacent spaces. The interval between the bass and the middle note is always a third (major or minor, depending on the type).
What are the four types of triad?
1) Major: major third + perfect fifth (C–E–G). 2) Minor: minor third + perfect fifth (A–C–E). 3) Diminished: minor third + diminished fifth (B–D–F). 4) Augmented: major third + augmented fifth (C–E–G♯). Each type has its own characteristic sound.
Why do we study root position first?
It is the most basic and recognizable form of a chord. It lets you learn how each type of triad looks before you meet the inversions, which rearrange the notes but keep the same chord. Root position is the reference from which every other position is analyzed.
How can I tell a major triad from a minor triad in root position?
Look at the interval between the bass (the root) and the middle note (the third). A major third has 4 half steps, and the chord is major. A minor third has 3 half steps, and the chord is minor. The fifth (the top note) is perfect in both cases.
