Key Signatures

Recognize any key at a glance

Being able to name the key of a piece just by looking at the sharps or flats at the start of the staff (the key signature) saves a lot of guessing. Once you know the trick, you do not need to analyze a single note.

These exercises are built around two visual shortcuts that let you find the key in a fraction of a second. First, memorize the order in which accidentals always appear.

The fixed order of sharps and flats

Memorize these two sequences as one block each. They are the order in which accidentals are always written on the staff:

  • Order of sharps (♯): F - C - G - D - A - E - B
  • Order of flats (♭): B - E - A - D - G - C - F (exactly the reverse of the sharps)

1. The shortcut for key signatures with sharps

When the key signature has one or more sharps, the method is almost automatic:

  1. Look at the last sharp (the one farthest to the right, just before the music begins).
  2. Go up a half step (UK: semitone) from the name of that note.
  3. That note is the name of the major key.

Example: The key signature has three sharps (F♯, C♯, G♯). The last one is G♯. A half step above G♯ is A, so the key is A major.

2. The shortcut for key signatures with flats

With flats, the shortcut is even quicker, because you do not have to count any distance on the keyboard:

  1. Find the second-to-last flat in the key signature (the one just to the left of the last flat).
  2. That flat, with its own name, is the name of the major key.

Example: The key signature has four flats in this order (B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭). The second-to-last is A♭, so the key is A♭ major.

The one exception: this shortcut does not work with a single flat, because there is no second-to-last. A key signature with one flat (B♭) is always F major.

Type of key signature Shortcut for the major key How to find the minor key (relative)
Sharps Last sharp + a half step up = major tonic. Go down a minor 3rd (3 half steps) from the major tonic.
Flats The second-to-last flat is the major tonic (exception: one flat = F major). Go down a minor 3rd (3 half steps) from the major tonic.

Every major key has a relative minor key that shares the same notes and the same key signature but has a different character. Once you know the major key, go down a minor 3rd (a whole step plus a half step) to get the name of its relative minor.

The next step is to practice neighboring keys: finding out which keys can be reached easily by modulation.

Once you can recognize keys on paper, try recognizing them by ear. In the ear-training exercises recognize the major key, recognize the minor key and recognize the key (major and minor), you hear an A4 and then a melody, and you write its key with the same builder used in the neighboring keys exercise.

Playing scales with the online metronome builds muscle memory. The online tuner lets you check that the altered notes of each key signature are in tune.

Key signature worksheets to print (PDF)

The same two skills as above, on paper and with an answer sheet: one worksheet to identify the key of a written key signature and another to write the key signature of a key you are given. Each sheet has 21 exercises on a single side of A4, and everything goes from easier to harder:

  • From few accidentals to many. They start with key signatures of one or two accidentals and end with those of six and seven.
  • Treble clef first. The bass clef appears from the second third onward, once the mechanism has been picked up.
  • Alternating major and minor. Each exercise says which of the two to give, so you have to go through the relative key.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I identify the key of a piece from its key signature?
The sharps or flats at the start of the staff show the key. With sharps: go up a half step from the last sharp to get the major key. With flats: the second-to-last flat is the name of the major key. Exception: a single flat always means F major.
What is the shortcut for key signatures with sharps?
Take the last sharp in the key signature (the one farthest to the right) and go up a half step. That gives the major key. Example: 3 sharps (F♯, C♯, G♯) means the last is G♯, a half step up is A, so the key is A major.
What is the shortcut for key signatures with flats?
The second-to-last flat gives the name of the major key directly. Example: 4 flats (B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭) means the second-to-last is A♭, so the piece is in A♭ major. If there is only one flat (B♭), the key is always F major.
What is a relative minor key?
Every major key has a relative minor with the same key signature. To find it, go down a minor 3rd (a whole step and a half step) from the major tonic. For example, the relative of C major is A minor; both have no sharps or flats.
How many keys are there to learn?
Students usually work on 30 keys: 15 major and 15 minor. There are 15 different key signatures (no accidentals, 7 with sharps and 7 with flats), and each one belongs to one major key and its relative minor.
