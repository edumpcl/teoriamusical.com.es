Build the Whole-Tone Scale

The name of a whole-tone scale appears and you write its 7 notes on the staff, from the tonic to its octave, each one a whole step (UK: tone) above the previous one and with the right accidental.

How to use the exercise: click (or drag) on the staff to place each note, in the order you hear it. Before placing it, choose the accidental with the ♭ ♮ ♯ buttons if the note needs one. Undo removes the last note and Clear starts again. The magnifier shows an enlarged view of the note you are about to place.

Six notes a whole step apart

The whole-tone scale splits the octave into six equal whole steps: it has no half steps at all, and so no leading tone either. Written in full, from the tonic to its octave, it has seven notes.

From C: C · D · E · F♯ · G♯ · A♯ · C.
From D: D · E · F♯ · G♯ · A♯ · B♯ · D.

One letter is always left out

Six notes cannot fill the seven letters of the octave, so somewhere one letter is skipped: those two notes form a diminished 3rd, which sounds the same as a whole step. In the example from D, B♯–D skips C. The most common spelling is the one above, where the scale moves through consecutive letters and skips at the end (1 · 2 · 3 · ♯4 · ♯5 · ♯6 · 8). When that would need a double sharp, another letter is skipped earlier: from C♯, C♯ · D♯ · F · G · A · B · C♯.

That is why the exercise accepts any correct spelling: the scale must start and end on the tonic, each note must be a whole step above the previous one and no letter may repeat. If you write another valid spelling, the correction also shows you the most common one.

The full theory, with the scale written from all 12 tonics, is on the page whole-tone scale. To practice the scale that moves by half steps instead, try building the chromatic scale.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you write a whole-tone scale?
From the tonic, go up six whole steps in a row until you reach the octave: from C, C D E F♯ G♯ A♯ C. Since there are only six notes for seven letters, one letter is skipped somewhere; the usual spelling moves through consecutive letters and skips at the end.
Is a hexatonic scale the same as the whole-tone scale?
The whole-tone scale is a hexatonic scale (six notes per octave), but not every hexatonic scale is whole-tone: the blues scale, for example, also has six notes. In Spanish-language conservatories "escala hexátona" or "hexatónica" usually means the whole-tone scale.
Why does the whole-tone scale have no leading tone?
Because all its intervals are whole steps: there is no half step, and the leading tone is the note a half step below the tonic. Without it the scale sounds suspended, with no clear tonal center.
How many whole-tone scales are there?
Only two different ones: the one that contains C (C D E F♯ G♯ A♯) and the one that contains C♯ (C♯ D♯ F G A B). Any other is one of these two starting on another note, written with its own spelling.
