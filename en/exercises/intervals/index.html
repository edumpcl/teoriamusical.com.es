Frequently Asked Questions

What interval exercises does this section include?
Exercises to identify the number (2nd to octave), the quality (major, minor, perfect, augmented, diminished), the direction (ascending or descending), the arrangement (harmonic or melodic), consonance or dissonance, half steps and enharmonic notes. There are also exercises to build intervals on the staff from the requested type.
How many types of musical intervals are there?
There are melodic intervals (notes in sequence, forming a melody) and harmonic intervals (notes at the same time, forming harmony). They are classified by number (2nd to octave) and by quality (perfect, major, minor, augmented, diminished).
How do you calculate the number of an interval?
Count the lines and spaces from the lower note to the upper note, including both. From C to G: C (1), D (2), E (3), F (4), G (5), so it is a 5th. Then count the half steps (UK: semitones) to find the quality.
In what order should you practice the interval exercises?
Start with the number of the interval, then its quality (by counting half steps), then consonance and dissonance, then direction and arrangement, and finally construction. Within each group, practice the simplest intervals first (2nds, 3rds) and move on to the more complex ones (7ths, augmented and diminished).
What is the difference between a diatonic and a chromatic half step?
A diatonic half step joins two notes with different letter names (E–F, B–C). A chromatic half step joins two notes with the same letter name and different accidentals (C–C♯, F–F♯). On the staff, the diatonic one occupies a new position, and the chromatic one stays on the same line or space.
