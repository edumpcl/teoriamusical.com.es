Roman Numeral Analysis Exercises

In this exercise you practice Roman numeral analysis: writing each chord with the Roman numeral of its scale degree in the key, followed by the figures that show its position and special notes. You get two exercises in one: identify the figures of a written chord, or build the chord from its figures. The levels progress by type of chord: triads in root position, inverted triads, seventh chords in root position, inverted seventh chords and everything mixed.

How Roman numerals are written

The label of a chord has two parts: the Roman numeral, which says which degree it is built on, and the figures, stacked to its right, which say what notes are above the bass. They are read like this:

  • The Roman numeral is always written in upper case (I to VII) and is the scale degree the chord is built on. Whether the chord is major, minor or diminished comes from the key and shows in the notes, not in the sign of the numeral.
  • No figure is a triad in root position. 6 is the first inversion and 6/4 the second. Seventh chords use the figure 7 and their inversions 6/5, 4/3 and 4/2 (in this site's method they are figured differently, as you will see below).
  • The + sign marks the leading tone: the note a half step below the tonic. On V it is the third of the chord; on VII it is the root.
  • A struck-through number is a diminished interval. The struck-through 5 shows the diminished fifth, and struck-through numbers are reserved for diminished chords.

To review how chords are built, see triads and seventh chords, where their figures are explained with images.

The chords of C major

A triad is built on each degree of the major scale. In root position, I, II, IV and VI have no figure; V carries the + of the leading tone and VII the struck-through 5 of its diminished fifth:

FiguresNotesChord
IC – E – Gmajor triad
IID – F – Aminor triad
IVF – A – Cmajor triad
V+G – B – Dmajor triad
VIA – C – Eminor triad
VII5B – D – Fdiminished triad

In a minor key: the leading tone

In minor keys the figures are worked out with the harmonic minor scale: the 7th degree is raised a half step, to have a leading tone. That is why V is major and VII is diminished, even though the key signature does not carry that accidental. In A minor the leading tone is G♯, and on the 2nd degree the fifth is already diminished. These are the chords of A minor:

FiguresNotesChord
IA – C – Eminor triad
II5B – D – Fdiminished triad
IVD – F – Aminor triad
V+E – G♯ – Bmajor triad
VIF – A – Cmajor triad
VII5G♯ – B – Ddiminished triad

The III chord is not asked: depending on the context it is major or augmented, and an exercise with two possible answers would not be fair. You can review the harmonic minor scale on its page.

Inverted triads

In inversions, the figures say which intervals are above the bass. The first inversion is figured with a 6 and the second with 6/4, and the leading tone is marked with the + on whichever note it is. Examples in C major:

ChordFiguresNotes
I · 1st inversionI6E – G – C
I · 2nd inversionI64G – C – E
V · 1st inversionV6B – D – G
V · 2nd inversionV+64D – G – B
VII · 1st inversionVII+63D – F – B
VII · 2nd inversionVII6+4F – B – D

Look at the first inversion of VII: the +6 says that the leading tone is a sixth above the bass, and the 3 below tells it apart from the second inversion of the dominant seventh.

The seventh chords in the exercise

The levels with seventh chords include the three that are figured in this site's method: the dominant seventh (on V), the leading-tone seventh (on VII of a major key) and the diminished seventh (on VII of a minor key). Their figures are the same in any key. With the leading-tone seventh of C major and the dominant seventh:

ChordFiguresNotes
V7 · root positionV7+G – B – D – F
V7 · 1st inversionV65B – D – F – G
V7 · 2nd inversionV+6D – F – G – B
V7 · 3rd inversionV+4F – G – B – D
VII7 · root positionVII75B – D – F – A
VII7 · 1st inversionVII+65D – F – A – B
VII7 · 2nd inversionVII+43F – A – B – D
VII7 · 3rd inversionVII4+2A – B – D – F

The diminished seventh has its own figures: the struck-through 7 and 5, +6 and a struck-through 5, +4 and ♭3, and +2. They are explained in dominant seventh, leading-tone seventh and diminished seventh.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is the Roman numeral always written in upper case?
Because the Roman numeral only says which degree the chord is built on. Whether the chord is major, minor or diminished comes from the key and can be seen in the notes; what marks it in the figures is, for example, the + of the leading tone or the struck-through 5 of the diminished fifth.
What does the + sign mean in the figures?
The + marks the leading tone, the note a half step below the tonic. On V it is the third of the chord (figure +), on VII it is the root, and in inversions the + goes with the figure of the note that is the leading tone: for example +6 or +4.
What is a struck-through number?
A struck-through number shows a diminished interval, like the struck-through 5 of the diminished fifth of the VII chord. Struck-through numbers are reserved for diminished chords and are not used in others.
Why is the III chord not asked?
Because its quality depends on the context: in a minor key it is major with the natural scale and augmented with the harmonic scale. An exercise should not have two valid answers, so the III chord is studied separately.
What is the difference between Roman numerals and chord symbols?
Roman numerals talk about degrees and depend on the key: V7 is the dominant seventh in any key. Chord symbols (C, Am, G7) name the chord by its root and do not depend on the key. Roman numerals are what you use to analyze the function of each chord.
