A tuner for every instrument

Tuner for guitar

Standard guitar tuning (E–A–D–G–B–E) is the most common. Turn on the microphone, pluck an open string and adjust until the meter sits in the center. It also works for Drop D (strings 1 to 5 as usual, the 6th lowered to D) and other alternate tunings, as long as the note is within the chromatic range. The tuner detects any note automatically, so you do not need to select the string first.

Tuner for bass guitar

A standard 4-string bass is tuned E–A–D–G, one octave below the four lowest strings of the guitar. The tuner detects low frequencies down to 41 Hz (E1), so it covers 4-, 5- and 6-string basses. For the best results with very low notes, play near an external microphone or turn up the volume of the instrument.

Tuner for violin

The violin is tuned in fifths: G–D–A–E. The A string (A4 = 440 Hz) is the usual reference at the start of a rehearsal. The A4 reference can be adjusted, so you can match a Baroque ensemble (often 415 Hz) or an orchestra that tunes higher. Play each open string and adjust until the needle is centered.

Tuner for ukulele

A standard soprano ukulele is tuned G–C–E–A (re-entrant tuning: the G string is higher than the C string). The tuner covers that range and recognizes every string with no setup. Turn on the microphone, play each string separately and check that the needle settles on the right note.

Tuner for viola

The viola is tuned in fifths: C–G–D–A (C3–G3–D4–A4), a fifth below the violin. Play each open string and adjust until the tuner shows the right note centered at 0¢. The lowest string, C3 (about 131 Hz), needs the instrument close to the microphone because the frequency is low.

Tuner for cello

The cello is also tuned in fifths, C–G–D–A, but an octave below the viola (C2–G2–D3–A3). The low C2 (about 65 Hz) is hard for a phone’s built-in microphone to pick up. If the tuner does not respond, move the instrument closer or use an external microphone. The usual tuning reference in an orchestra is the A of the oboe.

Tuner for wind instruments

Flute, oboe, trumpet and clarinet can be tuned with the microphone in the same way as string instruments. For transposing instruments (B♭ clarinet, B♭ trumpet, English horn in F, E♭ alto saxophone…) turn on the “Transposing instrument” box: you will see the sounding note and the written note, as it appears in the score, at the same time.

Understanding tuning

What is a cent?

The cent is the unit used to measure how far a note is from perfect tuning. A half step (semitone) is divided into 100 cents, so an octave has 1,200. Most listeners can hardly hear differences smaller than about 5 cents, so this tuner calls a note “in tune” when it is within ±5¢. The meter runs from −50¢ (half a half step flat) to +50¢ (half a half step sharp).

Frequency, hertz and pitch

The pitch of a sound depends on its frequency, measured in hertz (Hz): the more vibrations per second, the higher the sound. Every note has a specific frequency, and each octave up doubles it (A4 is 440 Hz; A5 is 880 Hz). The tuner converts the detected frequency into a note name and its deviation in cents. Octave numbers follow scientific pitch notation, in which middle C is C4.

A = 440 and calibration

The international standard sets A4 at 440 Hz, but it is not universal: many orchestras tune to 442 or 443 Hz to sound “brighter”, and early-music groups often use a Baroque pitch of 415 Hz. That is why the tuner lets you calibrate the A4 reference: set it before you begin so that it matches your ensemble.

Harmonics: why the fundamental matters

A real sound is not a single pure frequency. It is a fundamental (the one that gives the note its name) plus a series of higher harmonics that define the tone color. Simple tuners sometimes “jump” to a harmonic and show the wrong note. This tuner uses the YIN algorithm, which is robust against harmonics, to follow the fundamental even in rich or low sounds.

About accuracy

The real accuracy of any microphone tuner depends on the quality of the microphone, the background noise and how clean the sound is. Tested with exact tones, the detection engine (YIN) finds the frequency with an error of under about 1 cent across the musical range in clean-signal conditions; in real use, your microphone sets the limit. That is why there is a confidence indicator: if the signal is weak or noisy, the reading is not reliable, so move closer to the microphone and play a clear, sustained note. The stability history shows whether you hold the pitch or waver, which is very useful for voice and strings.

Frequently asked questions

How does the online tuner work?
Press the microphone button and allow audio access in your browser. When you play a note, the tuner detects the musical note (C, D, E…) and how many cents it is from perfect tuning. Adjust the tension until the meter is centered at 0¢.
Is this tuner free?
Yes, it is 100% free. There are no subscriptions, payments or downloads, and no limit on use. It runs in your browser, on a phone or a computer.
Which instruments can I tune?
Any instrument that produces a tone with a clear fundamental: guitar, bass, violin, viola, cello, ukulele, mandolin, flute, oboe, trumpet, clarinet and more. For transposing instruments (B♭ clarinet, E♭ alto saxophone…) turn on the “Transposing instrument” box to see the written note and the sounding note at the same time.
What are cents, and what are they for?
A cent is one hundredth of a half step (semitone). The tuner shows how many cents the note is from perfect tuning, on a scale from −50¢ to +50¢. A negative value means the note is flat, so tighten the string. A positive value means it is sharp, so loosen it. Perfect tuning is 0¢.
Can I change the reference A?
Yes. In the “A4 reference” box you can set A4 in steps of 1 Hz. Most ensembles use a value between 435 Hz and 450 Hz: the international standard is 440 Hz, but many orchestras use 442 Hz or 443 Hz. The tuner also goes down to 390 Hz and up to 480 Hz, which covers Baroque pitch (about 415 Hz). Change the value before you start tuning.
Does it work on a phone?
Yes. It works in Chrome, Safari, Edge and Firefox on Android, iPhone, Windows and Mac. The browser only needs access to the device’s microphone.
